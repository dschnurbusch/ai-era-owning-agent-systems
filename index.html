01 / The thesis

Owning is the setup,
not the server.

01

Skills

The playbooks. How your firm actually does the work, written so an agent can follow them.

02

Tools

Which systems you choose to connect, and which ones stay closed.

03

Data

What information may leave the firm, and where it is allowed to go.

04

Approvals

The rule for what may be sent, filed, paid, or deleted, and who says yes.

These are the parts a client would recognize as yours.

A year of running an agent harness taught the distinction. The server was rented attention. The setup is the practice: procedure, judgment about which tools to open, control of client information, and a human yes on anything that leaves the building. A firm can rent the model and the computer and still own those four.

03 / Built into the case system

If the work lives on the matter, start there.

Filevine · Lois

On the matter.

Lois works inside Filevine. It can read the matter and write work back to the system of record, for a lawyer to review.

Clio · Vincent

On the matter.

Vincent works inside Clio Work. It researches and drafts, and with a matter open it can act in Clio Manage. A lawyer still reviews the output.

Ryan McKean is right to favor buying this ready-made. I want that case in the room.

When the recurring job already lives in Filevine or Clio, the agent that sits on that record is a serious starting point. Lois and Vincent are built around the matter, the deadline, and the document, and both vendors describe a lawyer remaining in the loop. I am not going to talk a firm out of a tool that fits. Read the current Filevine and Clio pages before you rely on a feature list from this slide. Marketing language moves faster than the product.

04 / One honest lesson

I already ran
the hard version.

HERMES · AN OPEN-SOURCE HARNESSI stood it up because I wanted to own the system.
What I owned was the maintenance.

If that trade is not worth it for me, I have a hard time naming the firm it is for.

Hermes is a real open-source agent harness, and I ran one. The lesson is personal and narrow. Standing up the software is the part that looks like ownership. Keeping connectors, secrets, updates, and a person on call is the part that consumes a practice. I am not offering a setup guide, and I am not scoring other people's harnesses. Other open harnesses exist. The hours land in the same place.

04 / Then we leave it

The server was
the small part.

HarnessThe piece that was easiest to admire.
The real workConnectors, secrets, updates, and whoever answers when it stops.
The modelStill rented, unless you take that project on too.
What keptThe procedures. They moved. The server did not earn its keep.

One minute on this. The rest of the hour is the easy path.

Self-hosting the harness does not keep client information on a machine you control. The model can still be a cloud service, and every connected system is another destination. Hosting also does not make the software free. Someone pays in hours. I am not publishing a cost figure, because a single firm's hours would be a story, not a benchmark. The direction was clear enough that I stopped.

06 / Tools you choose

Click Connect.
Sign in.

  1. 01Open Connect apps.
  2. 02Choose Add on the app.
  3. 03Sign in with that provider.

In the chat, type @ to attach the app to a task.

The login stays off the computer. The bot calls the tool and does not receive the token.

Every bot on your account can use a connected app. A teammate's Team Bot asks before it uses your accounts. Prefer the app over clicking through the website.

Documented path: Connect apps, Add, then the provider's own login. OAuth tokens are held on the connector side. The bot invokes the tool without receiving the token. Apps are account-wide, so connecting Gmail on a personal account connects it for every bot you run. A Team Bot belonging to a teammate asks before it uses your accounts. Blocking an app in team policy does not, by itself, block that service's website. Connect two apps in week one. Leave the rest closed until a person has watched the pilot.

07 / Skills and playbooks

Point the bot
at a skill.

A skill is the procedure: when to use it, the steps, how to check, and what needs a person.

Type / to call one. One library, shared by your bots. Turn a skill on for the bot that should use it. Save it after the task works once.

SKILL / waiting-on-the-client 1. Read the open task. 2. Read the message and the attachment. 3. List only what is still missing. 4. Draft the reply. Do not send it. 5. Return the draft and the sources. Stop if authority is unclear.

Public docs describe a skill as a reusable set of instructions: when to use it, the inputs, the sequence, how to validate, what to return, and what requires approval. Private skills are one library across your bots, enabled per bot. Packaged skills can be installed from Connect apps. Teach-by-demonstration exists where that control is shown; if it is not on your account, write the skill in words. The example on the slide is the fictional demo skill. Keep a copy in your own files so the procedure can move to another platform. A file is not a guarantee that the next product will enforce it. Test the stop rule there too.

08 / A schedule you can say

Tell it when,
in a sentence.

“Every weekday at 8:00, run the follow-up skill on the sample list. Post the drafts here. Send nothing. If the file is missing, stop and tell me.”

Confirm six things: the bot, the clock and time zone, the source, the result, the approval line, and what happens when the source is missing. A test run does real work. It can run with the laptop closed.

You create a routine by asking the bot that should own it. The docs ask you to confirm the owner, schedule and time zone, input, expected result, approval boundary, and the no-data rule. Schedules are at least five minutes apart. A bot can own up to 50 routines, and the app keeps the 20 most recent runs of each. Deleting a routine does not ask twice. Event triggers, where supported, include Slack and GitHub, and they need a narrow match. “Every new message” is how a routine becomes noise. Write the skill first. Automate it after a second successful run. Background routines continue while the laptop is closed, which is a reason to keep sending behind an approval.

09 / A design choice

A computer that
stays on.

The computer

Browser, files, terminal.

Work continues after the laptop closes. You can open the screen and watch it.

The screen

One per bot.

Each bot has its own screen, so several can work at the same time.

One computer per account. Screens are separate.
Files and sign-ins are shared. A second bot is not a wall around a client's file.

The product overview says each bot works on a persistent cloud computer with a browser, a filesystem, and a terminal. The security docs are the sentence a firm needs: all of your bots share one computer assigned to the user account. Each bot gets its own screen. Screens are separate work surfaces, not separate security boundaries. Between users, isolation is hardware-level. Inside one account, a file or a signed-in session is available to every bot. Do not place a credential or a client file on that computer if another bot should not see it. Deleting a bot does not wipe the shared files or the browser sessions. Your own laptop is a different machine. A bot runs there only if you allow it, and the default is to ask every time.

10 / Passwords

The bot signs in.
It never sees the password.

Vault, or 1Password

You approve the login.

1Password fills a login you approve. Connecting it does not open the rest of the vault.

Hidden from the bot

Password. Code. Card number.

You can also take over the computer and type the step yourself.

Vault is the whole account, not one bot. A saved card can be used at checkout without a fresh approval. Leave the firm card out if you want a yes on every charge.

Cursor's Vault help says the bot never sees a saved password, card number, or security code. It can see contact info and which logins exist, because it has to choose an entry and type an address. 1Password's partner page says the same thing about passwords and one-time codes: the bot requests a specific login, you approve it, and the connection is not access to the entire vault. Signing in still lets the bot see what that website shows. Vault needs a current Grok Bot app, is account-wide, and is not supported on Team Bots. There is no setting that forces a fresh approval every time a saved card is used. For filing fees and trust money, type the payment yourself or leave the card out of Vault.

11 / Approval rules

Draft the message.
Ask before it sends.

The card

Allow once. Always allow. Deny.

The conversation shows the proposed action and what it will touch. Read the target before you answer.

Auto Review

A check before it runs.

Ask-first rules always stop the action. If an allow rule also matches, ask-first wins.

Automatic review is a backstop.
The lawyer still reviews the work.

The product is built around drafts you approve before they are sent. Put that in the skill and in an Ask-first rule for external email, messages, filing, payment, deletion, and anything that changes a production system. Auto Review evaluates tool calls and computer actions before they run. It is model-based. The docs say it should sit alongside least privilege, not replace it. An approval from a routine expires after about ten minutes if you do not answer, and the action does not run. Always-allow saves a matching rule, so make the rule narrow. An approval does not undo work the bot already did. Set the Ask-first rule before you connect a real inbox.

12 / A result you can look at

Ask for a page
you can check.

01

A report

What came in, what is still missing, and the source for each line.

02

A tracker

Open tasks, owners, and status, in one place beside the chat.

03

A routine

The same page can be refreshed on a schedule you can read.

04

A boundary

On a self-serve plan, a shared page can be public. Keep client information off it.

A Page is a styled report, dashboard, or tracker the bot builds beside the chat. It can hold tables, charts, and task lists. You can talk to the bot to change the page, and a routine can update it. Self-serve plans can share a page with the public. Enterprise plans can share with specific users, groups, or teams. For a law practice, treat a page like a document you might hand someone: fictional or internal operating data is fine on a demo, client information is not a candidate for a public link. Find earlier pages from search or the side panel.

13 / A roster

Give each job
a bot.

  • Research
  • Ops
  • Finance
  • Marketing
  • Leads
  • Mailroom
  • Home
  • And others

That list is mine. The idea is a name, a job, and a boundary.

Use a group chat when the handoff should be visible. The split organizes the work. You already heard the limit: on one account, they share the computer.

The docs describe a bot as a durable teammate with a name, a job, its own conversation, and context that builds over time. Create a separate bot when the goal, the tools, the style, the approval line, or the schedule is actually different. “General helper” is a weaker design than “mailroom.” My working roster includes Research, Ops, Finance, Marketing, Leads, Mailroom, Home, and others. It is an operating choice, not a template to copy. Bots can hand work to each other in a group chat. They do not create confidentiality walls. Memory is continuity, not the system of record. Changing facts stay in the matter file.

15 / The duties that do not move

You still own
the duties.

Confidentiality

Rule 1.6. Know where the information goes, who can see it, whether it trains a model, and how long it is kept. Know this before a real matter is connected.

Supervision

Rules 5.3 and 1.1. You check the output. Competence includes understanding the tool you are supervising. The bot is not a member of the bar.

The yes

Send, file, pay, and delete wait for a person. Put that in the skill and in an ask-first rule. A sentence in a prompt is not the control.

The slide uses ABA Model Rule numbering, which is what a national room expects. Missouri numbers the same duties 4-1.6, 4-5.3, and 4-1.1. ABA Formal Opinion 512 (2024) is the longer generative-AI discussion: confidentiality, competence, and supervision of nonlawyer assistance. This slide is a discussion prompt for firm owners, not an opinion on anyone's particular stack. Read the rule text in your own jurisdiction, and read the vendor's current terms, before client information moves.

16 / Portability, vendor risk, and what is coming

Carry the skill.
Watch the vendor.

A copy you can move

Keep the playbook in your own files, in plain language. Test it on the next platform before you need to switch.

The account terms

Grok Bot stores data and does not offer Legacy Privacy Mode. Training choices follow the account's privacy settings. Read them while the pilot is still fake.

Coming

Phone calls, and private-network reach such as Tailscale, are coming. They are not this month's workflow.

Skills inside a product are convenient and sticky. A plain-language copy, of the kind in the demo folder, is what you can hand to Claude, to ChatGPT, to Vincent, or to the next agent that can read a procedure. The open Agent Skills format is one way to structure that file. Storage in the product is not the same thing as an export guarantee. On vendor risk: Grok Bot uses the Cursor account's privacy settings, requires data storage, and does not support Legacy Privacy Mode. Review retention, subprocessors, and what happens to connected apps if you close the account. Voice chat inside the app is a separate, shipped control. A phone call the bot places or answers is the piece to treat as coming. Enterprise materials describe a Tailscale pattern for team computers. For this room, call it coming. Do not build a solo firm's week-one plan on it.

17 / A plan for a lawyer

Four moves.
Then stop and look.

01

Pick one tool

Lois or Vincent, if the job lives in that system. A commercial agent, if it crosses systems.

02

Connect two apps

A calendar and a notes tool, or a demo mailbox. Leave the rest closed.

03

Write one skill

One process, with the steps, the stop, and the action that waits for a person.

04

Add one routine

A weekday draft. Sample data. Nothing sent until you have watched it twice.

The worksheet in the guide is this list, with room to write.

Pick the tool for the job you already have, not the tool you might want in a year. Two connections are enough to learn how approval feels. The waiting-on-the-client skill in the demo folder is a lawful template: swap in your own nonconfidential example before any client record. Run the routine once by hand, then on a schedule, and read every draft. Measure whether the draft was usable, not whether the bot sounded confident. Expand authority after that, one action at a time.
